## Research Strategy Critique and Direction-Finding Prompt

**Objective**
Critically evaluate the current body of evidence, reports, interpretations, and strategic plans for this research project, and produce a grounded set of justified research directions and recommendations. Your goal is not to defend the existing framing, but to determine what is actually true, what is only weakly supported, what may be misframed, and what work is highest value next.

**Epistemic stance**
Assume the provided reports may contain useful observations but flawed interpretation, overclaiming, framing bias, local myopia, or premature convergence. Treat prior writeups, summaries, strategic plans, and “headline” claims as hypotheses to stress-test, not conclusions to inherit. Apply scientific skepticism throughout.

Important distinction:
- The underlying data and experimental artifacts may be trustworthy.
- The interpretation of that data may be wrong, incomplete, overfit to a narrative, or strategically misguided.

Do **not** anchor on:
- existing headlines
- prior storylines or dichotomies
- previous “recommended next steps”
- the user’s own framing
- the fact that multiple prior analyses may converge on the same interpretation

If a prior framing is strong, it should survive adversarial scrutiny. If it does not, say so clearly.

**Core task**
Using all provided sources and evidence, produce an adversarial-but-fair research strategy analysis that answers:

1. What findings currently appear genuinely robust?
2. Which claims are fragile, underidentified, overstated, confounded, or dependent on hidden assumptions?
3. Which framing choices are narrowing thinking or creating false dichotomies?
4. What alternative interpretations or higher-value narratives better fit the evidence?
5. What research directions are actually highest leverage from here, given the constraints?
6. What should be deprioritized, postponed, reframed, or discarded?

**Method**
Work from first principles and evidence hierarchy, not rhetoric. Separate:
- observations
- interpretations
- causal claims
- external validity claims
- strategic recommendations

For every major conclusion, explicitly consider:
- alternative explanations
- measurement artifacts
- confounders
- selection effects
- benchmark/task dependence
- framing dependence
- what evidence would falsify the conclusion

Look for:
- hidden assumptions
- false tradeoffs
- premature narrowing onto one narrative
- local optimizations that hurt long-term research value
- recommendations that are superficially plausible but low information gain
- places where the project is underselling or overselling its actual contribution

Do not assume the best framing is one of the framings already proposed. The correct synthesis may be:
- a hybrid
- a third framing
- a narrower but stronger claim
- a broader but differently structured research program

**Decision standard for recommendations**
Recommend directions based on explicit reasoning, not vibe. Rank or evaluate options using criteria such as:
- expected information gain
- impact on the project’s core scientific contribution
- robustness under scrutiny
- reviewer/paper/fellowship value
- marginal cost in time, compute, and money
- dependency structure / sequencing value
- risk of producing ambiguous results
- fit with the long-term research agenda, not just short-term optics

Prefer work that clarifies the project’s true contribution, resolves key ambiguities, or materially strengthens the most decision-relevant claims.

**Constraints**
Use the actual project constraints provided, including:
- time horizon
- deadline reality vs nominal deadline
- available compute / budget / credits
- current experiments in progress
- what can be executed locally vs remotely
- the fact that the project may continue iterating after submission
- the strategic importance of this work for future opportunities

Do not optimize only for immediate deadline submission if that would distort the longer-term best path. Equally, do not ignore the need for incremental deliverables under real constraints.

**Required output**
Structure the answer into these sections:

### 1. Evidence-grounded state of play
A concise description of what the evidence currently supports, with clear separation between strong findings, tentative findings, and unresolved areas.

### 2. Framing audit
Identify the main framing problems in the existing reports/plans:
- where they are biased, narrow, or overanchored
- where they create false dichotomies
- where they miss the more interesting scientific question
- where they are strategically myopic

### 3. Adversarial critique of current conclusions
Stress-test the dominant interpretations. Explain which ones survive scrutiny, which do not, and why.

### 4. Alternative research narratives or strategic frames
Propose the best candidate framings of the project given the evidence. These may include a refined main story, parallel publishable stories, or a different decomposition of the work.

### 5. Recommended research directions
Give a prioritized set of next steps. For each:
- what question it answers
- why it matters
- what uncertainty it resolves
- expected upside
- expected downside / risk
- cost and feasibility
- whether it is urgent now, useful later, or not worth doing

### 6. What to stop, postpone, or de-emphasize
Be explicit about low-ROI work, seductive but weak directions, and tasks that feel productive but do not materially improve the contribution.

### 7. Strategic execution plan
Provide a phased plan:
- immediate / deadline-adjacent
- next 1–2 weeks
- longer-horizon extensions

This plan should balance incremental delivery with long-term research taste.

### 8. Final judgment
Give your best overall view of what this project most plausibly is, what contribution it can realistically make, and how to maximize its scientific and career value from here.

**Style requirements**
- Be rigorous, skeptical, and strategically minded.
- Do not flatter.
- Do not inherit prior framings unless they earn it.
- Make uncertainty explicit.
- Distinguish clearly between “well-supported,” “plausible,” and “speculative.”
- Prefer precise criticism over generic advice.
- Be willing to discard most of the prior plan if warranted.
- Equally, be willing to defend parts of it if they genuinely survive scrutiny.
